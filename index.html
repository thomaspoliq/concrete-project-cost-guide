Concrete Project Cost Guide

The True Cost of a Concrete Project: A Complete Budget Guide

Ask most people what a new driveway or patio costs and they will quote you the price of the concrete. That figure is real, but rarely more than half of the final bill. Delivery charges, groundwork materials, formwork, tools, sealer and getting rid of the dug out soil all add up, and they are exactly the costs that catch first time budgets out. This guide lists every item a complete project pays for, with typical price ranges, and builds them into one worked example.

The short answer: For a typical self managed project, expect the concrete itself to account for roughly 40 to 50 percent of the total spend. The rest goes on delivery and possible short load fees, sub base material, formwork timber, reinforcement where needed, tool hire or purchase, sealer, and waste disposal. Budget for all of them up front, add a contingency of about 10 percent, and the final invoice will hold no surprises. All prices in this guide are typical approximate UK figures to guide a budget, not quotations.

The concrete itself is only the starting point

Ready mix is sold by the cubic metre (or by the cubic yard in the United States), and the rate varies with the strength grade, the region and the supplier. Higher strength mixes cost a little more per unit, and prices move over time with fuel and cement costs, so check the current concrete cost per cubic yard and per cubic metre rates before you finalise any budget. Bagged concrete looks simpler but works out far more expensive per cubic metre, which is why bags only make sense for small volumes. Whichever way you buy it, remember that the price printed on the concrete is the price before it travels anywhere or lands on prepared ground, and that is where the rest of the budget lives.

Delivery, short load fees and pump hire

Mixer trucks carry around 6 to 8 cubic metres. Suppliers price their rates around full loads, so ordering a small quantity often triggers a part load or short load charge to cover the wasted capacity and the same journey cost. Typical part load fees run from about £30 to £75 on top of the concrete price, and some suppliers add a charge if the truck is kept waiting on site beyond the agreed unloading time. Ask about both fees when you book, because they are rarely in the headline rate.

If the truck cannot get close to the pour, the concrete travels by wheelbarrow, which is slow and limits how much you can place before it stiffens. A line pump solves this: the machine parks at the kerb and pumps the concrete through a hose to exactly where it is needed. Typical hire for a pump with an operator runs from about £250 to £400 for a half day session, and on awkward sites it often pays for itself in saved labour and a better finish.

Groundwork costs: sub base, formwork and reinforcement

The slab only performs as well as what sits underneath it, and groundwork materials are the second biggest block of spending after the concrete itself.

None of these items is glamorous, but skipping them is a false economy. A slab laid on uncompacted soil settles unevenly and cracks, and putting it right costs several times the groundwork you saved.

Finishing, sealing and clearing up

The last group of costs arrives at the end of the job and is the easiest to forget. Finishing tools such as a magnesium float, an edging tool and a broom for a brushed finish can be bought for roughly £40 to £90 in total, or the larger items hired. A penetrating or film forming sealer protects the surface from stains, frost and de icing salts: a typical 25 litre drum costs around £60 to £100 and covers roughly 4 to 6 square metres per litre once diluted into two coats, so most driveways need one drum. Finally, the soil and rubble you dug out has to go somewhere. A typical 8 yard skip costs about £200 to £300 including the hire period, and grab hire can be better value for larger volumes of clean spoil. Your own labour is free, but price in food and drink for the friends you recruit; it is the cheapest insurance on the whole project.

Worked example: a budget for a typical driveway

Take a front driveway 8 m by 5 m, which is 40 square metres, laid 100 mm thick on 100 mm of sub base. The slab volume is 40 x 0.1, which is 4 cubic metres of concrete, and the sub base needs roughly 8 tonnes of Type 1. Building the full project budget from the items above gives this picture:

Two lessons stand out from this table. First, the concrete at £440 to £560 is well under half of the total, which is exactly why budgets built on the concrete price alone fall apart. Second, none of the supporting items is individually huge; the money leaks away across eight or nine modest lines, so the only reliable defence is a complete list like this one, costed before you start.

Frequently asked questions

Why do quotes for the same job vary so much?

Because quotes include different things. One contractor may price concrete and labour only, expecting the existing base to be reused, while another includes excavation, sub base, steel and waste removal. Compare quotes line by line against a full list like the one in this guide rather than by the headline figure.

Is it cheaper to mix my own concrete from bags?

Only for small jobs. A 20 kg bag yields about 0.009 cubic metres, so a cubic metre takes over 100 bags, and at typical bag prices that costs several times the ready mix rate, plus a mixer hire and a great deal of labour. Beyond roughly half a cubic metre, ready mix is normally cheaper as well as better.

What is a short load fee and can I avoid it?

It is the charge suppliers add when you order less than a full truck load, because the journey and the wasted drum space still cost them the same. You can sometimes avoid it by combining your pour with a neighbour's project, or sizing the job to a full load where that is practical.

How much contingency should I add?

About 10 percent on top of your itemised total is a sensible allowance. It covers the extra half tonne of sub base, the second drum of sealer, a longer skip hire if the weather delays you, and the small fixings and materials runs that every project generates.

Does reinforcement always add to the cost?

Only where it is needed. Light duty paths and patios on firm ground can work without steel, but driveways, garage floors and any slab over filled ground benefit from mesh, and it is cheap insurance at roughly £25 to £45 a sheet compared with the cost of lifting and relaying a cracked slab.

When is a pump worth paying for?

When the truck cannot reach within a short barrow run of the pour, when the volume is large enough that barrowing would let the concrete stiffen before it is placed, or when access crosses finished gardens or surfaces that barrow traffic would damage. On open front driveways with good access, most people manage without one.

Conclusion

A realistic concrete budget is a list, not a single number. Start with the concrete at current per unit prices, then add delivery and any short load fee, the sub base and compaction, formwork, reinforcement where the job needs it, finishing tools, sealer, and waste disposal, and finish with a 10 percent contingency. Do that and your project total will land within a few pounds of the plan, which is more than can be said for budgets that only ever counted the concrete.
